Rover wheel design for lunar regolith

Northwestern Lunabotics · Research Team Lead · March 2026–present

Terramechanics Material selection Wear testing Systems engineering

Problem

Lunar regolith is abrasive in a way terrestrial soil is not. Its particles are angular, unweathered, and electrostatically active, and a wheel that performs well in sand will wear rapidly in regolith simulant. For NASA's Lunabotics Challenge the wheel has to deliver traction in a low-cohesion medium while surviving sustained contact with it, and those two requirements pull material selection in opposite directions.

Approach

I lead a ten-person research subteam covering wheel design and terramechanics, working with Northwestern faculty advisors. The work spans grouser geometry, traction modeling in low-cohesion media, and screening of polymer and composite candidates for abrasion resistance in regolith simulant.

Carbon-fiber reinforcement was the team's initial candidate for the contact surface, on the reasoning that a stiffer, stronger composite would last longer. Wear testing showed the opposite: the reinforcement degraded abrasion performance rather than improving it. On that result I redirected the design toward a decoupled architecture — a carbon-fiber structural core for stiffness, with unfilled nylon at the surfaces that actually contact regolith.

Results

  • Reversed the team's material direction on the strength of wear-test evidence, replacing a reinforced contact surface with a decoupled core-and-surface design.
  • Authored two systems engineering reports and design specifications adopted for the competition rover.
  • Own the research timeline and design review deliverables for the season.
